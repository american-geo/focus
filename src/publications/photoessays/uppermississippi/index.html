A River Almost Ran Through It - Turning Frequent Flooding into Opportunities for Experiential Learning

Ranbir Kang, Department of Geography and Anthropology, Kennesaw State University
Roger Viadero, Institute for Environmental Studies, Western Illinois University
DOI: 10.21690/foge/2026.69.5p
Figure 1: Map of the Upper Mississippi River (UMR) with Lock and Dam locations, Twin Cities, Quad Cities, and St. Louis.

The construction of the locks and dams (Figure 1 and Figure 2) that dominate the Upper Mississippi River (UMR) were viewed by many as a symbol of progress and engineering achievement that would enhance the prosperity of the nation. Constructed in the 1930s and 40s by the US army Corps of Engineers, a series of twenty-nine lock and dam structures created a stairway of water from the Twin Cities in Minnesota to St. Louis, Missouri (https://experiencemississippiriver.com/locks-and-dams-of-the-upper-mississippi/).

Figure 2: Students inside Mississippi River Lock 19, Keokuk, IA (photo by Ranbir Kang).

While the project provided the statutorily required 2.7 m (9.0 ft) deep navigation channel (https://www.mvp.usace.army.mil/Home/Projects/Article/571102/upper-mississippi-river-9-foot-channel-navigation-project-locks-and-dams) needed to support waterborne commerce, backwaters and side channels were flooded, islands disappeared, and valuable aquatic and terrestrial habitats were lost. In turn, this impacted the pattern of development along the river. For example, communities along the UMR have constructed levees and floodwalls (Figure 3, top and bottom) to protect their people and property from rising river waters. These water diversions prompted other communities to follow suit by building ever larger and more numerous structures to protect property from flooding damage. However, little thought was put into mitigating the net effects of these structures on the broader function of the river (https://levees.sec.usace.army.mil/). The result of these efforts is a patchwork of structures that channelize and divert water. By the late twentieth century, engineers, planners, and the general public began to recognize the need to change our approach to managing these water resources and adjacent environments. In the twenty-first century, the need for new approaches continues to be informed and challenged by the impacts of global climate change. This work is one example of the ways that students can become actively engaged in providing informed responses to this pressing challenge.

The Quad Cities region (Illinois and Iowa; https://visitquadcities.com/) is home to more than 470 thousand people in Moline and Rock Island, Illinios, and Bettendorf and Davenport, Iowa, and many smaller neighboring communities (Figure 4). To serve this population, Western Illinois University opened the doors of its riverfront campus (https://www.wiu.edu/qc/) in August 2014 (Figure 5 and Figure 6). Notable features of the facility include geothermal heating and cooling, vegetated “green” roofs, and the extensive use of recycled and renewable materials in its construction. The facility earned Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design (LEED; https://www.usgbc.org/leed) Silver Certification by the U.S. Green Building Council. Based on its location adjacent to the Mississippi River (Figure 7), the campus was designed to remain open during periodic flooding events. This aspect of the design was challenged in 2019 when record-setting flooding impacted the Upper Mississippi River Basin (UMRB) from March through June.

In January 2019, the Quad Cities region received 73.2 cm (28.8 in) of snow which is 5.4 cm (2.2 in) greater than the previous record set in January 1979. In response to intense spring rainfall onto frozen or saturated soils, regional waterways began what would be a record setting period of flooding across the UMRB. In March 2019, major rivers in the UMRB began to flood surrounding communities. By the end of the month, the Mississippi River spilled out of its banks in Moline, Illinois, and began migrating toward WIU’s riverfront campus. By early April, riverfront structures were inundated by floodwaters and local roads became part of the river (Figure 8 and Figure 9). On April 2, 2019, an observation site was established at the curb of River Drive, immediately in front of campus, to study the depth of the floodwater (Figure 10 and Figure 11). This fixed location was identified by feel with a surveying rod as the floodwater became deeper.

This effort began as an opportunity for Environmental Science Ph.D. students to gain hands-on experience conducting field investigations under significant time and resource constraints. However, Master’s degree students from the Department of Earth, Atmospheric, and Geospatial Sciences (EAGIS), undergraduate student interns, and a homeschooled student quickly joined the effort in capacities ranging from data collection, photo documentation, GIS analysis, and the creation of outreach materials that are shared with the public. These data, as well as information gathered in subsequent floods, have also been integrated into graduate-level coursework on watershed-scale environmental assessment and management.

The water level measured on River Drive fluctuated from ~31 to 125 cm (~1.0 to 4.1 ft) between April and June 2019 (Figure 11 and Figure 12). On May 2, 2019, the Mississippi River in Pool 15 rose to its peak stage of 6.92 m (22.7 ft)—2.13 cm higher than the previous record set on July 9, 1993 (https://rivergages.mvr.usace.army.mil/WaterControl/stationinfo2.cfm?sid=MI15&fid=RCKI2&dt=S). On River Drive, this corresponded to a water depth of 125 cm. These conditions required the team to be mindful of obvious sources of floating debris as well as unseen materials that were transported along River Drive (Figure 13). Students also reached out to community members to obtain aerial imagery of the campus during the flood (Figure 14). They surveyed the edge of water and debris fields and noted the extent of water at key locations on campus (Figure 15). While surveying campus during the flood, the team encountered a variety of debris (Figure 16, Figure 17, and Figure 18). While there was significant water intrusion onto campus property, and temporary flood barriers were erected, no buildings were flooded (Figure 19 and Figure 20). Campus facilities remained free of water due to proactive steps taken during the design process including the use of a floating foundation which allowed buildings to be built with flood protection in mind.

In 2023, the campus was impacted by another flood and a new generation of environmental researchers put on their waders and continued the work of their predecessors (Figure 21). After considering data from 2019, the team noted a linear relationship between the water depth on River Drive and the river stage (Figure 22) reported by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for Pool 15. Using 2019 data, the team was able to predict the 2023 floodwater levels (2.5% difference between model and measured values) at River Drive based on river stage. When combined with field notes on the water level at key campus locations, campus managers can now make informed flood response decisions based on river stage data. For example, when the river stage at Lock and Dam 15 reaches 4.88 m, River Drive in front of campus is closed and all campus traffic must be accommodated on the access road to the south. When the river stage reaches, 5.60 m, the river-facing half of the east parking lot must be closed.

Summary

Students and faculty at WIU’s riverfront campus in Moline, Illinois, have turned the liability of frequent Mississippi River flooding into an opportunity for significant experiential learning. As opposed to idly watching floodwater levels rise, two generations of students have responded to this challenge by obtaining hands-on experience studying flood processes. Their unique experiences include implementing strategies to work safely around floodwaters, the use of modern survey instrumentation, the acquisition and geoprocessing of aerial imagery, and the development of a management tool that can be used to plan a response to rising floodwaters.
